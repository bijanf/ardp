## 3.5 SSS Trend Decomposition: Anthropogenic Forcing vs Ocean Dynamics

### 3.5.1 Atlantic SSS trends are not explained by the water cycle

The "salty gets saltier, fresh gets fresher" amplification hypothesis (Held & Soden, 2006; Durack & Wijffels, 2010) predicts that greenhouse-driven intensification of the hydrological cycle should amplify the climatological SSS pattern: net evaporative regions (high mean SSS) should salinify, while net precipitative regions (low mean SSS) should freshen. If this mechanism dominates, SSS trends should scale linearly with mean SSS.

We test this prediction across the Atlantic basin using GLORYS12V1 (1993–2025, 396 monthly fields). After deseasonalizing the monthly SSS fields and computing per-pixel OLS trends, we regress zonal-mean SSS trends against zonal-mean climatological SSS across 25 latitude bands (5° width, 55°S to 70°N). This zonal-mean approach averages out mesoscale noise and tests the large-scale meridional structure.

The regression yields R² = 0.06 and p = 0.24 — the amplification relationship is **not statistically significant** in the Atlantic. The hydrological cycle intensification, while a real global phenomenon, explains less than 10% of the variance in Atlantic zonal-mean SSS trends. This stands in stark contrast to the Pacific and Indian Oceans, where amplification accounts for a much larger fraction of SSS variability (Durack et al., 2012).

### 3.5.2 Three processes that break the amplification pattern

The failure of the amplification model is not random scatter — it is structured. Three physically distinct deviations emerge, each pointing to active ocean circulation changes:

**South Atlantic salinity pile-up.** The subtropical South Atlantic (35°S–15°S) shows observed salinification of +0.084 PSU decade⁻¹, while the amplification model predicts only +0.030 PSU decade⁻¹ based on the region's moderate mean SSS (~35.5 PSU). The deviation of +0.054 PSU decade⁻¹ — representing 64% of the total observed trend — cannot be explained by evaporation-minus-precipitation changes. This excess salinification is consistent with reduced northward salt advection by a weakening AMOC: as the overturning slows, salt that would normally be exported northward accumulates in the South Atlantic (Zhu & Liu, 2020; Latif et al., 2022).

**Tropical freshening.** The equatorial Atlantic (10°S–5°N) freshens more strongly than the amplification model predicts. While the tropics are already relatively fresh (mean SSS ~35 PSU), the observed freshening exceeds what enhanced precipitation alone would produce. This is consistent with a southward shift or intensification of the Intertropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ), which alters the freshwater input pattern across the tropical Atlantic. Changes in ITCZ position are themselves linked to the interhemispheric temperature gradient created by differential AMOC heat transport.

**Subarctic salinification.** The high-latitude North Atlantic (60°–70°N) shows salinification despite being a region of low mean SSS (~34.5 PSU), where the amplification model predicts freshening. This anomalous signal is consistent with enhanced advection of warm, salty Atlantic water into the Nordic Seas and Arctic — a process driven by changes in the subpolar gyre circulation and potentially by Arctic sea ice retreat exposing the ocean surface to increased evaporation.

### 3.5.3 The residual map: isolating the AMOC fingerprint

The pixel-level residual field (observed trend minus amplification-predicted trend) provides a spatial map of where ocean dynamics override the hydrological cycle. The subtropical South Atlantic stands out as a broad region of strong positive residuals, spatially coincident with the salinity pile-up index region defined by Latif et al. (2022). The subpolar North Atlantic shows negative residuals, consistent with reduced poleward salt transport by the upper AMOC limb. The zero-residual contour roughly follows the inter-gyre boundary near 40°N, separating the accumulation zone to the south from the depletion zone to the north — a pattern diagnostic of reduced meridional overturning.

### 3.5.4 Implications for attribution

The failure of the amplification test in the Atlantic has a direct implication for the attribution debate. If Atlantic SSS trends were primarily a passive response to greenhouse-forced changes in the water cycle, the amplification regression would be strong (high R², significant slope). Instead, the weak relationship (R² = 0.06) demonstrates that ocean circulation changes — not the atmospheric water cycle — are the dominant driver of Atlantic salinity redistribution over the satellite era.

This does not mean the water cycle is irrelevant. The amplification signal is present globally and contributes a baseline trend. But in the Atlantic, circulation dynamics add a much larger signal on top, reorganizing salt in ways that the water cycle alone cannot explain. The coexistence of both signals — a weak but positive amplification baseline overlaid by strong dynamical deviations — argues against interpreting Atlantic SSS changes as purely internal variability. The hydrological component is anthropogenic (greenhouse-forced E-P intensification), and the dynamical component (AMOC weakening) is a response to the same forcing through Greenland melt and surface buoyancy changes.
